Chapter 1 · 自然地理 / Natural Geography (241 words)

01atmosphere/ˈætməsfɪə(r)/ BrE · /ˈætməsfɪr/ NAmEnoun · B1
Definition
1. (the atmosphere) the mixture of gases that surrounds the earth · 2. a mixture of gases that surrounds another planet or a star · 3. the air in a room or in a small space · 4. the feeling or mood that you have in a particular place or situation.
  • Wind power doesn't release carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallTwo quite different meanings live inside one short word. The earth has an atmosphere (no article in BrE writing, the atmosphere as a singular noun); a room has an atmosphere (countable, can be smoky/stuffy). Don't write “pollution of atmospheres” — the natural one is uncountable.
📖 Oxford: atmosphere
02hydrosphere/ˈhaɪdrəsfɪə(r)/noun
Definition
All the water on the earth's surface, including oceans, seas, rivers, lakes and the water vapour in the air; the water component of the earth.
  • About 71% of the hydrosphere is contained in the oceans.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallDon't pronounce the y as in “why” — it is /haɪ/ as in “high”, and the stress is on HY-dro-sphere. Also: no plural. The earth has one hydrosphere.
📖 Oxford: hydrosphere
03lithosphere/ˈlɪθəsfɪə(r)/noun
Definition
The rigid outer part of the earth, including the crust and the upper part of the mantle; broken into the tectonic plates that carry the continents.
  • The lithosphere is divided into about fifteen major tectonic plates.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress is on LITH-o-sphere, and the th is the soft sound as in “thin”, not “the”. Don't write lithosphere with an s after the t (liths-… is a common misspelling).
📖 Oxford: lithosphere
04oxygen/ˈɒksɪdʒən/ BrE · /ˈɑːksɪdʒən/ NAmEnoun
Definition
A colourless, odourless chemical element (symbol O) that exists as a gas in the atmosphere and is essential for respiration in most living things.
  • Oxygen makes up roughly 21% of the earth's atmosphere.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallBrE /ɒksɪdʒən/ vs NAmE /ɑːksɪdʒən/ — the first vowel is a different vowel. And oxygen is uncountable in nearly every natural use: “a breath of fresh oxygen” sounds wrong. Say “fresh air” or “more oxygen”.
📖 Oxford: oxygen
05oxide/ˈɒksaɪd/ BrE · /ˈɑːksaɪd/ NAmEnoun
Definition
A compound formed when oxygen combines with another chemical element.
  • Iron oxide is the chemical name for rust.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallTwo common traps: (1) Say /ˈɒksaɪd/ “ox-ide”, not /ɒkˈsaɪd/. The stress is on the first syllable. (2) Oxide names in chemistry: “carbon oxide” is rare; the everyday word is carbon monoxide (CO) or carbon dioxide (CO₂). Don't mix them up in Writing Task 1.
📖 Oxford: oxide
06carbon dioxide/ˌkɑːbən daɪˈɒksaɪd/ BrE · /ˌkɑːrbən daɪˈɑːksaɪd/ NAmEnoun
Definition
A colourless, odourless gas (chemical formula CO₂) produced by burning carbon-containing material, by breathing, and by natural processes; the main greenhouse gas from human activity.
  • Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallWriting “CO2” is fine in informal notes, but in an essay write CO₂ (subscript) or, simpler, just spell out carbon dioxide. And remember it has two syllables in di-oxide: /daɪˈɒk/, not /diˈɒk/.
📖 Oxford: carbon dioxide
07hydrogen/ˈhaɪdrədʒən/noun
Definition
A colourless, odourless, very light gas (symbol H) that combines with oxygen to form water; the most common element in the universe.
  • Hydrogen is the simplest and most abundant element in the universe.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallBrE drops the h in connected speech: /ˈhaɪ-drə-dʒən/ → sounds almost like “high-drij'n”. Don't insert an extra dr and say “hyd-ro-gen” (it is hy-dro-gen: /haɪ-drə-/).
📖 Oxford: hydrogen
08core/kɔː(r)/noun · adj.
Definition
1. the hard central part of a fruit, especially an apple. 2. the central part of the earth, or of any object. 3. the most important or basic part of something.
  • The earth's core is divided into a liquid outer core and a solid inner core.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallDon't say “corse” or “kor” — the vowel is the long /ɔː/ in “more / call / law”. And be careful with spelling: it is core, not core as in “corps” (which is a different word meaning a military unit).
📖 Oxford: core
09crust/krʌst/noun
Definition
1. the hard outer layer or surface of bread, a pie, etc. 2. a layer of something hard, especially one that forms on top of a softer or liquid thing. 3. (geology) the outer layer of the earth.
  • The earth's crust is broken into tectonic plates.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallThe plural is crusts (with -s, not “crusties” or “crusts” as in “rusts”). And don't confuse crust (the hard outer layer) with crustacean (a sea animal with a shell) — they share a root but mean different things.
📖 Oxford: crust
10mantle/ˈmæntl/noun · verb
Definition
1. (geology) the part of the earth between the crust and the core. 2. a loose, sleeveless cloak worn over other clothes. 3. a covering layer of something. 4. (figurative) a role or responsibility taken on by someone.
  • The upper mantle is solid rock; the lower mantle flows very slowly.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallSpelling trap: mantle (the layer) and mantel (the fireplace shelf) are spelled differently in BrE. Also: mantle is pronounced /ˈmæntl/ — the final e is silent, not “mantle-ee”.
📖 Oxford: mantle
11longitude/ˈlɒŋɡɪtjuːd/ BrE · /ˈlɑːŋɡɪtuːd/ NAmE · /ˈlɒndʒɪtjuːd/ BrE alt.noun
Definition
The distance of a place east or west of a line that runs from the North Pole to the South Pole through Greenwich, London, measured in degrees.
  • Sydney is at longitude 151° east, latitude 34° south.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallDon't pronounce the t in long-i-tude like a t; the t becomes /tʃ/ in BrE (long-i-chude) and stays /t/ in NAmE (long-i-tood). The stress is on LONG.
📖 Oxford: longitude
12latitude/ˈlætɪtjuːd/ BrE · /ˈlætɪtuːd/ NAmEnoun
Definition
1. the distance of a place north or south of the equator, measured in degrees. 2. (figurative) freedom to choose what to do or say.
  • The region lies at a latitude of about 50° north.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallMany learners say “longtitude / longtitude” by mixing up longitude and latitude. A safer pair to drill: long → north–south (pole-to-pole); lat → east–west (like rungs on a ladder).
📖 Oxford: latitude
13altitude/ˈæltɪtjuːd/ BrE · /ˈæltɪtuːd/ NAmEnoun
Definition
1. the height above sea level. 2. (usually plural) a place that is high above sea level.
  • We are flying at an altitude of 6 000 metres.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallAltitude ≠ attitude. They differ by one letter but mean very different things: altitude = height; attitude = a way of thinking. Don't write “a positive altitude to life” — that's attitude. BrE /ˈæltɪtjuːd/ (with the extra y) vs NAmE /ˈæltɪtuːd/.
📖 Oxford: altitude
14horizon/həˈraɪzn/noun
Definition
1. the line at which the earth's surface and the sky appear to meet. 2. (usually plural, figurative) the limit of a person's knowledge, experience or interest.
  • The sun sank below the horizon.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress is on the second syllable: /hə-RAI-zn/. Don't say “HOR-i-zon”. And the noun is usually singular in the literal sense (“the horizon”) but plural in the figurative sense (“broaden your horizons”).
📖 Oxford: horizon
15disaster/dɪˈzɑːstə(r)/ BrE · /dɪˈzæstər/ NAmEnoun
Definition
1. an unexpected event, such as a flood, storm or accident, that causes great damage or loss of life. 2. (informal) a complete failure; a very bad person or thing.
  • Earthquakes, floods and typhoons are all natural disasters.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallBrE and NAmE pronunciation differ on the second vowel: BrE /dɪˈzɑːstə/ (long “ah” as in “bath”); NAmE /dɪˈzæstər/ (“za” as in “fast”). Pick one and stick to it in the exam — mixing them inside one answer is jarring.
📖 Oxford: disaster
16mishap/ˈmɪshæp/noun
Definition
A small accident or piece of bad luck that is not very serious.
  • We arrived home without mishap.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress is on MIS-hap (the first syllable), not “mi-SHAP”. And: mishap is much more polite / humorous than accident in writing. A sentence like “the meeting passed without mishap” is a standard, slightly old-fashioned formal English.
📖 Oxford: mishap
17catastrophic/ˌkætəˈstrɒfɪk/ BrE · /ˌkætəˈstrɑːfɪk/ NAmEadjective
Definition
1. (of a natural event) causing many people to suffer. 2. causing a lot of problems for people.
  • The region was hit by a catastrophic earthquake.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress pattern: cat-a-STRO-phic — the main stress is on STRO, not on “cata-”. The ph in the middle is /f/, not /p/ (it's from Greek -phikos). A very common pronunciation mistake: saying “cat-as-TRO-fik” with the last syllable wrong.
📖 Oxford: catastrophic
18calamity/kəˈlæməti/noun
Definition
An event that causes great damage, suffering or loss; a disaster, especially one that affects many people.
  • The flood was the worst natural calamity in the country's history.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress: ca-LAM-i-ty (the second syllable is loudest). The plural is calamities /kəˈlæmətiz/. Don't write “calamy” or “calamitys”.
📖 Oxford: calamity
19endanger/ɪnˈdeɪndʒə(r)/verb
Definition
To put somebody or something in a situation in which they could be harmed, lost or destroyed.
  • The chemical spill endangered the local water supply.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallThe prefix is en- (as in enrich, encourage), not in- (as in inside). Don't write “indanger”. And endangered is what you want when you talk about species — not “dangerous species”, which means species that are themselves a threat.
📖 Oxford: endanger
20jeopardise/ˈdʒepədaɪz/verb
Definition
To put something important such as a plan, a job or a relationship at risk of being harmed or lost.
  • A single mistake could jeopardise her career.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallSpelling: British jeopardise / American jeopardize. The OALD main headword is the US spelling jeopardize. For your exam, use one spelling consistently. And jeopardise is a stronger, more literary verb than endanger — reserve it for careers, reputations and big plans, not for spilled coffee.
📖 Oxford: jeopardise
21destructive/dɪˈstrʌktɪv/adjective
Definition
Causing damage or harm; wanting or tending to destroy things.
  • The hurricane was one of the most destructive in decades.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress: de-STRUC-tive (the second syllable is the loudest). The ct cluster in destructive is hard to say for many learners — practise “de-STRUCK-tiv” slowly first.
📖 Oxford: destructive
22El Niño/el ˈniːnjoʊ/ NAmE · /el ˈniːnjəʊ/ BrEnoun
Definition
A climate pattern in which the surface water of the eastern Pacific Ocean becomes unusually warm, affecting weather in many parts of the world; the opposite phase is called La Niña.
  • El Niño events occur every two to seven years.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallIt is two words, capital E capital N, with a tilde over the n in formal writing: El Niño. Pronounce it /el NEE-nyoh/. The plural in normal English is El Niños; in Spanish it is El Niños too (Spanish plurals keep the article in this phrase).
📖 Oxford: El Niño
23greenhouse/ˈɡriːnhaʊs/noun · adj.
Definition
1. (noun) a building made mainly of glass, used for growing plants that need warmth and protection. 2. (as modifier) of or producing the greenhouse effect: greenhouse gas, greenhouse warming.
  • Tomatoes are grown in greenhouses all year round.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallIt's one word: greenhouse (no hyphen). The plural in “two greenhouses” adds a regular -s, but the compound noun “greenhouse gas” uses singular gas as a category: “a greenhouse gas” is fine; “greenhouse gases” also fine in the plural sense.
📖 Oxford: greenhouse
24phenomenon/fəˈnɒmɪnən/ BrE · /fəˈnɑːmɪnən/ NAmEnoun
Definition
1. a fact or an event in nature or society, especially one that is not fully understood. 2. a person or thing that is very successful or impressive. · Plural (sense 1): phenomena /fəˈnɒmɪnə/; (sense 2, NAmE): phenomenons.
  • Globalisation is a phenomenon of the 21st century.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallOne of the most-frequently-misused words in English. Phenomena is plural; using it as a singular (“a phenomena”) is the classic giveaway. Memorise the trio: criterion / phenomenon / bacterium all follow the Greek pattern -on → -a.
📖 Oxford: phenomenon
25pebble/ˈpebl/noun
Definition
A small, smooth, round stone, especially one found on a beach or in a river.
  • The beach was covered in smooth pebbles.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallThe consonant cluster pb- in pebble is a pronunciation trap. The b is silent: /ˈpebl/, not “peb-bl”. The plural is pebbles (two syllables, /ˈpeblz/).
📖 Oxford: pebble
26magnet/ˈmæɡnət/noun
Definition
1. a piece of iron or other material that can attract iron or steel. 2. (figurative) a person, place or thing that attracts many people.
  • The fridge was covered with tourist magnets.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallThe g in magnet is silent: /ˈmænət/, not “mag-net”. The same applies to magnetic /mæɡˈnetɪk/ — the first g is silent, the second is pronounced.
📖 Oxford: magnet
27ore/ɔː(r)/noun
Definition
Rock or earth from which metal or valuable minerals can be obtained.
  • The region is rich in iron ore.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallDon't confuse ore /ɔː/ with oar /ɔː/ (a paddle) and or /ɔː/ (the conjunction). They are homophones. In writing, context is enough: “ore” always appears in a geology/ mining context.
📖 Oxford: ore
28mineral/ˈmɪnərəl/noun · adj.
Definition
1. (noun) a solid, naturally occurring substance that is not a plant or animal, such as gold, salt or quartz. 2. (BrE, plural, often minerals) = mineral water: “a glass of minerals” (BrE). 3. (adjective) of or relating to minerals: mineral deposits / mineral water.
  • Iron and copper are essential minerals for industry.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallIn BrE, the plural minerals can mean mineral water at a restaurant (“two sparkling minerals, please”). NAmE speakers do not use this short form — say mineral water in full.
📖 Oxford: mineral
29marble/ˈmɑːbl/noun · adj.
Definition
1. a hard, usually white or patterned stone, used for buildings and statues. 2. (usually plural) a small glass ball used in children's games. 3. (adjective) made of marble; figurative: cold or smooth like marble.
  • The Parthenon is built of marble.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallTwo unrelated meanings share one spelling. In the geology sense, marble is uncountable: “a slab of marble”. In the toy sense, the plural is marbles — it is also a name for the game: “Let's play marbles.” The adjective sense is rare and literary.
📖 Oxford: marble
30quartz/kwɔːts/noun
Definition
A hard mineral, often in the form of clear or coloured crystals, used in making watches, clocks and electronic circuits.
  • The watch keeps time using a tiny piece of quartz.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallSpelling: quartz has no e after the z (unlike German Quarz). Pronunciation: /kwɔːts/, ending in the cluster /ts/. The plural is quartz (uncountable) or quartzes in technical writing. Don't write “quarts” — that means a unit of volume.
📖 Oxford: quartz
31granite/ˈɡrænɪt/noun
Definition
A very hard, usually grey rock, used for buildings and monuments.
  • The monument is carved out of solid granite.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress is on GRAN-ite. Don't say “gran-EET” or “GRAN-it-eh” — the ending is just one short syllable /ɪt/. The t is fully pronounced.
📖 Oxford: granite
32breeze/briːz/noun · verb
Definition
1. (noun) a light, gentle wind. 2. (informal, countable) something that is easy to do. 3. (verb) to move quickly and lightly.
  • A cool breeze blew in from the sea.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallDon't confuse the noun breeze /briːz/ (light wind) with the verb to breeze through (to do something easily). They share a root but the figurative verb is informal — do not use it in IELTS Writing.
📖 Oxford: breeze
33gust/ɡʌst/noun · verb
Definition
1. (noun) a sudden, short burst of wind, rain, sound, or an emotion. 2. (verb) (of wind) to blow in gusts.
  • A sudden gust of wind blew the hat off her head.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallPronounce the final consonant: /ɡʌst/, not “gus”. And the figurative use — “a gust of laughter”, “a gust of nostalgia” — is a high-frequency IELTS essay phrase.
📖 Oxford: gust
34gale/ɡeɪl/noun
Definition
1. a very strong wind; on the Beaufort scale, a gale is roughly force 8–10. 2. (figurative) a sudden loud burst of noise, e.g. laughter.
  • The coast was battered by gale-force winds.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallDon't rhyme it with “pale” — it is /ɡeɪl/ like the man's name Gael / gale (rhymes with “fail” in some accents and “bell” in others — but the standard pronunciation is rhymes with “pale”).
📖 Oxford: gale
35hurricane/ˈhʌrɪkən/ BrE · /ˈhɜːrɪkeɪn/ NAmEnoun
Definition
A violent storm with extremely strong winds and heavy rain, especially in the western Atlantic Ocean. (Same phenomenon is called a typhoon in the western Pacific and a cyclone in the Indian Ocean.)
  • New Orleans was devastated by Hurricane Katrina in 2005.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallPronunciation: BrE /ˈhʌrɪkən/ (three syllables, stress on the first), NAmE /ˈhɜːrɪkeɪn/ (two syllables, rhymes with “cane”). Don't use the NAmE pronunciation in a BrE exam or vice versa — pick one and stick to it.
📖 Oxford: hurricane
36tornado/tɔːˈneɪdəʊ/ BrE · /tɔːrˈneɪdoʊ/ NAmEnoun
Definition
A violent storm with very strong winds that spin extremely fast and form a funnel-shaped cloud that touches the ground; common in central US in spring and summer. (Also called twister in informal NAmE.)
  • The tornado flattened the entire town in under a minute.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress: tor-NAY-do. Plural: tornadoes /tɔːˈneɪdəʊz/ (BrE also accepts tornados, but tornadoes is preferred). Don't write “tornadoes” as if it were Italian: “tornadi” is wrong.
📖 Oxford: tornado
37typhoon/taɪˈfuːn/noun
Definition
A violent tropical storm with very strong winds, occurring especially in the western Pacific Ocean. (Same phenomenon as a hurricane in the Atlantic / a cyclone in the Indian Ocean.)
  • The Philippines was hit by Typhoon Haiyan in 2013.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallThe spelling typhoon is sometimes mixed up with taifun or taifoon (German) or taifun. The English spelling is typhoon, two os, one ph. Stress: ty-FOON.
📖 Oxford: typhoon
38monsoon/ˌmɒnˈsuːn/ BrE · /ˌmɑːnˈsuːn/ NAmEnoun · C1
Definition
1. a period of heavy rain in summer in South Asia; the rain that falls during this period. 2. a wind in South Asia that blows from the south-west in summer, bringing rain, and the north-east in winter.
  • Travelling is much more difficult during the monsoon.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallPronounce the ns as /ns/, not /nz/: /mɒnˈsuːn/, not “mon-zoo-n”. The oo is a long /uː/ as in “moon”. And remember: it's not only rain. Monsoon is also the wind that brings the rain.
📖 Oxford: monsoon
39volcano/vɒlˈkeɪnəʊ/ BrE · /vɑːlˈkeɪnoʊ/ NAmEnoun
Definition
A mountain or hill with a hole at the top through which gases, hot liquid rock (lava) and ash can escape from deep inside the earth.
  • Mount Etna is an active volcano in Sicily.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallPronunciation: /vɒl-KAY-noh/ — stress on the second syllable, not the first. Plural: volcanoes (preferred) or volcanos. Don't write “volcanos” if you can avoid it — the standard plural is -oes.
📖 Oxford: volcano
40erupt/ɪˈrʌpt/verb
Definition
1. (of a volcano) to throw out burning rocks, ash and lava. 2. (of a person or feelings) to become suddenly and violently loud or active. 3. (of violence, fighting, etc.) to start suddenly.
  • The volcano erupted without warning, sending ash 10 km into the air.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallThe noun is eruption (one p before the -tion: rup → ruption). Don't write “eruption” as “eruption” — wait, the spelling is correct, but people sometimes write “eruption” correctly and then misspell the verb as “eruption” by analogy. The verb is erupt; the noun is eruption.
📖 Oxford: erupt
41magma/ˈmæɡmə/noun
Definition
Hot, liquid rock found below the earth's surface. When it reaches the surface it is called lava.
  • The magma chamber lies a few kilometres below the volcano.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallPronounce it /ˈmæɡmə/ — both syllables get full vowels, the g is hard, the final a is a schwa. Don't say “mag-ma” with a long second vowel, and don't drop the first g.
📖 Oxford: magma
42thermodynamic/ˌθɜːməʊdaɪˈnæmɪk/ BrE · /ˌθɜːrmoʊdaɪˈnæmɪk/ NAmEadjective
Definition
Relating to the branch of physics that deals with the relationship between heat, work, temperature and energy.
  • Engineers applied thermodynamic principles to design a more efficient turbine.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallStress: ther-mo-dy-NAM-ic (the third syllable is the loudest). The th at the start is the soft /θ/ as in “thin”, not the hard /ð/ as in “the”. Don't say “ther-mo-DIE-namic”.
📖 Oxford: thermodynamic
43smog/smɒɡ/ BrE · /smɑːɡ/ NAmEnoun
Definition
A form of air pollution that is a mixture of smoke and fog, especially in big cities.
  • The city was covered in a thick layer of smog.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallThe word is one syllable: /smɒɡ/ (BrE rhymes with “fog”). Don't insert an extra vowel and say “smog-ee”. The plural is unusual: smog is uncountable in standard usage.
📖 Oxford: smog
44fume/fjuːm/noun · verb
Definition
1. (noun, usually plural) smoke or gas that has an unpleasant smell, especially from a chemical or car engine. 2. (verb) to be very angry; to give off fumes.
  • The workshop was full of petrol fumes.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallIn BrE, fume rhymes with “flew” (/fjuːm/); in NAmE the /j/ sound can be reduced to /fjuːm/ or even /fuːm/. Don't say “fume” like “boom” — the final m is a real consonant.
📖 Oxford: fume
45mist/mɪst/noun · verb
Definition
1. (noun) a thin cloud of very small drops of water in the air, near the ground. 2. a film of moisture that makes it difficult to see clearly. 3. (verb) to cover or become covered with mist.
  • The hills were hidden by early morning mist.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallShort vowel: /mɪst/ like “missed”, not “moist” (which is a different word with a long vowel). The s and t form a final cluster, so the word is one syllable.
📖 Oxford: mist
46tsunami/tsuːˈnɑːmi/noun · B2
Definition
An extremely large wave in the sea caused, for example, by an earthquake under the sea.
  • A tsunami early-warning system was set up in Hawaii.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallPronounce it /tsuː-NAA-mi/ (stress on the second syllable). The initial ts is a single affricate sound, like in “cats” — don't break it into two syllables “t-su”. The plural is tsunamis /tsuːˈnɑːmiz/.
📖 Oxford: tsunami
47flooding/ˈflʌdɪŋ/noun
Definition
A situation in which an area of land is covered with water, especially when this causes damage; the action of being covered with water.
  • Severe flooding forced thousands to leave their homes.
⚠ Native-speaker pitfallFlooding is a noun formed from the verb flood; do not use it as an adjective. Say “flooded areas”, not “flooding areas” (which would mean “areas that are flooding something else” — wrong). Also: inundation is the very formal alternative if you need a Band 9.0 word.
📖 Oxford: flooding
48torrent/ˈtɒrənt/ BrE · /ˈtɔːrənt/ NAmEnoun
